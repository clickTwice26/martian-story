# Surviving Jezero Means Baking Rocks for Water

NASA data say humans can survive on Mars only by staying inside a pressurized, shielded, powered shell, and by producing their own oxygen and some of their water from local resources. Oxygen from the air is already proven in principle: Perseverance's MOXIE pulled **122 g of oxygen from Jezero's CO₂ atmosphere over 16 runs, peaking at 12 g/hr at ≥98% purity** ([NASA JPL](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-oxygen-generating-experiment-moxie-completes-mars-mission/)). Water and methane rocket fuel are much harder at Jezero, and the reason matters for the project's map. Jezero sits at about 18°N, well south of every confirmed deposit of shallow ice, and Perseverance's ground radar sees solid rock underneath. A Jezero crew would therefore have to bake water out of hydrated sulfates, clays and salty soil, then remove toxic perchlorate before drinking it. Radiation (about **0.64–0.67 mSv/day** on the surface), air pressure of about 7 mbar, swings of 50–60 °C between day and night, and dust storms lasting months all point to the same design: a habitat buried under roughly a metre or more of regolith and powered by a nuclear reactor, which NASA chose as its baseline Mars surface power in 2024. Everything above benchtop scale is still projected rather than demonstrated, and as of October 2026 no follow-on to MOXIE is funded. For "Martian Story," the most accurate framing is that Jezero is survivable but costly. It is rich in hydrated minerals and well studied, but it has no ice, so the map should show survival as a set of resource trade-offs, not a supply of easy water.

## Jezero has no ice, so water must be cooked out of minerals

The best-mapped water on Mars is buried ice in the northern mid-latitudes, not at Jezero. Dundas et al. catalogued **48 ice-exposing impact craters, all poleward of 35°N/S**, with ice under **0.42–0.90 m** of cover that is more than **95% ice by volume** ([Dundas et al. 2021, JGR Planets](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2020JE006617)). Eroding scarps show ice sheets more than **100 m thick** starting just 1–2 m below the surface ([Dundas et al. 2018, Science](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aao1619)). The lowest-latitude ice exposure known is a 150 m crater near **35°N**, formed by an impact in December 2021 ([USGS / Dundas et al. 2023](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70241592)). NASA's SWIM project maps likely ice no closer to the equator than about **30°N**, with the richest areas in Arcadia, northern Utopia and Deuteronilus Mensae ([Morgan et al. 2025, PSJ](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/ad9b24)). My arithmetic, at about 59 km per degree of latitude, puts the SWIM ice boundary at least **~690 km** north of Jezero and the nearest confirmed exposure at least **~980 km** north. Those are straight-north minimums; the confirmed crater lies at a different longitude, so the real distance is longer. JPL's own reasoning shows the tension: "If you send humans to Mars, you want to get them as close to the equator as you can," yet ice is "not thermodynamically stable at any depth at low latitudes" ([JPL](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasa-is-locating-ice-on-mars-with-this-new-map/); [JHU APL](https://www.jhuapl.edu/news/news-releases/170928-fresh-look-older-mars-data-yields-equatorial-surprise)). Perseverance's RIMFAX radar sees **low-porosity mafic rock** below the Séitah formation, not ice ([UCLA](https://www.college.ucla.edu/2022/08/25/mars-underground-images-from-perseverance-rover)).

What Jezero does have is water locked into rock. Perseverance found that every western-fan sandstone is hydrated and contains carbonates. The fan-front mudstones hold clays and **hydrated Mg/Fe sulfates reaching 20 wt% SO₃** ([Bosak et al., NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240000548/downloads/Bosak%20AbSciCon%202024.pdf)). The 2015 proposal to make Jezero a human Exploration Zone highlighted a **500 m thick stack of hydrous Mg-sulfate layers** and pre-Isidis basement rocks rich in water-bearing clays. Both lie within the zone's ~100 km radius ([Mustard et al., LPI abstract 1034](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/explorationzone2015/pdf/1034.pdf)). Plain soil also yields water: Curiosity's SAM instrument baked **1.5–3 wt% water** out of Gale Crater dust ([Leshin et al. 2013](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/s584q-ekz80)), and Odyssey neutron data imply **2–10% water-equivalent hydrogen** in equatorial regolith ([Feldman et al. 2004](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/global-distribution-of-near-surface-hydrogen-on-mars/)). There is a catch. Some Jezero sulfate is anhydrite, which contains no water ([arXiv 2410.05615](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.05615)), and no published study yet gives a measured water percentage for Jezero's own sulfates or clays. Any ore at Jezero would have to be prospected before it could be relied on.

NASA's Mars Water ISRU Planning (M-WIP) study shows how much the choice of feedstock matters. It sized a plant to produce **16 t of water over 480 sols**, mostly for ascent propellant, and compared the power each feedstock would need ([M-WIP report](https://sciences.ucf.edu/class/wp-content/uploads/sites/23/2020/04/Mars_Water_ISRU_Stud1.pdf)):

| Feedstock (M-WIP case) | Water content | Processing power | Rock handled in 480 sols | Energy per kg water (my calc) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gypsum / poly-hydrated sulfate @150 °C | ~20 wt% | **1.6 kW** | ~180 t | ≈1.2 kWh |
| Smectite clay @300 °C | ~4 wt% | 4.8 kW | ~580 t | ≈3.6 kWh |
| Typical regolith @150 °C | ~1.5 wt% | **7.4 kW** | **~2,020 t** | ≈5.5 kWh |
| Atmospheric water (210 ppm) | — | **~65 MW** | — | impractical |

The study concluded that, apart from drilling down into ice, a **poly-hydrated sulfate deposit is the most advantageous** water source. Ice would beat every mineral case if access problems can be solved ([M-WIP](https://sciences.ucf.edu/class/wp-content/uploads/sites/23/2020/04/Mars_Water_ISRU_Stud1.pdf)). This favors Jezero more than its lack of ice would suggest. A plant on Jezero's sulfate units would work in the cheapest mineral case, while a plant on generic soil would handle about **11 times more rock** for the same water. NASA Glenn's lab tests add a warning: an open tilt-tray reactor recovered only **about 50%** of the water in spiked simulant ([Trunek et al., NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20180002377/downloads/20180002377.pdf)). Real yields will fall short of what the minerals contain.

### Perchlorate makes Martian water poisonous until it is treated

Water extracted at Jezero would be toxic as produced. Phoenix measured **0.4–0.6% perchlorate** in polar soil ([Hecht et al. 2009](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/detection-of-perchlorate-and-the-soluble-chemistry-of-martian-soi/)), and Perseverance's PIXL instrument found Jezero fan-front regolith enriched to **2.0 ± 0.5 wt% chlorine** as chlorides, chlorates and/or perchlorates ([Astrobiology.com summary of JGR paper](https://astrobiology.com/2025/10/11/mineralogically-diverse-and-salt-rich-regolith-in-jezero-crater-characterized-using-x-ray-spectroscopy/)). Perchlorate blocks the thyroid from taking up iodide ([NASA NTRS 20190028297](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20190028297/downloads/20190028297.pdf)). Researchers at NASA Ames estimate that raw Martian water would carry about **10,000,000 ppb** each of perchlorate and chlorate, so it would need to be cleaned about **100,000-fold** ([Rothschild et al., LPSC 2025](https://epizodyspace.ru/bibl/inostr-yazyki/Lunar_and_Planetary_Science/2025/Rothschild_et_al_Detoxifying_Mars_56th_LPSC_no_2713_(2025).pdf)). A UC Riverside molybdenum catalyst removes **more than 99.99%** of perchlorate in the lab ([NSF](https://www.nsf.gov/news/new-water-treatment-earth-could-also-help-mars)). The likely engineering answer is to evaporate the water from heated rock and condense it elsewhere, leaving the salts behind in the hot residue, and then polish it. That is a reasonable inference from salt chemistry, but no NASA test has confirmed it. Perchlorate is not only a hazard: it also contains oxygen ([NASA NTRS 20150023545](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150023545/downloads/20150023545.pdf)).

Drinking water is a small demand compared with fuel. NASA's standard requires at least **2.5 L of potable water per crewmember per day**, plus 240 mL per hour of spacewalk ([NASA OCHMO](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/water-technical-brief-ochmo.pdf)). The ISS recovers about **98%** of its water ([NASA ECLSS fact sheet](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/g-657270-59-hp-environmental-control-and-life-support-system-eclss.pdf)). By my arithmetic, a crew of four recycling at ISS levels would lose only about **150 kg over a 500-sol stay**. Without recycling, a survivor drinking the 2.5 L minimum from 20% sulfate ore would need to dig about 25 kg of rock a day at the ~50% recovery seen in the Glenn tests, or about 330 kg a day from ordinary 1.5% soil. That gap is a strong story mechanic for where on the map a character goes to dig.

## MOXIE proved oxygen from air, at one two-hundredth of mission scale

MOXIE's results are the strongest survival evidence NASA has gathered on Mars itself. The 17.1 kg, 300 W unit ([NASA Science](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mars-2020-perseverance/science-instruments/)) filtered Jezero air, compressed it with a scroll pump, and heated it to **800 °C**. A scandia-stabilized zirconia electrolyte then split CO₂ into CO and oxygen ions, letting only the oxygen through ([Hoffman, Hecht et al. 2022, Science Advances](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9432831/)). It ran by day and night across a full Mars year at pressures of **~5–10 mbar**, and it beat its 6 g/hr requirement by a factor of two ([NASA JPL](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-oxygen-generating-experiment-moxie-completes-mars-mission/)). Its feedstock is the most reliable resource on Mars: Curiosity's multi-year average gives **95% CO₂, 2.59% N₂, 1.94% Ar and 0.161% O₂** ([Eos / Trainer et al. 2019](https://eos.org/research-spotlights/curiosity-rover-reveals-oxygen-mystery-in-martian-atmosphere)). Oxygen production therefore works the same at Jezero as anywhere else on Mars, and does not depend on ice.

The demonstration is far from mission scale. A person uses an average of **0.84 kg of oxygen a day** (range 0.49–1.25 kg) ([NASA NTRS 20120015343](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20120015343/downloads/20120015343.pdf)). By my arithmetic, that is about 35 g/hr, or three MOXIEs at peak rate running nonstop for each astronaut. MOXIE's lifetime output of 122 g would keep one person breathing for about 3.5 hours. Rocket fuel is the main oxygen demand. A six-person methane/oxygen Mars Ascent Vehicle would need about **31 t of oxygen and 9 t of methane**, so a full plant must make **2–3 kg/hr**, or "a couple of hundred times bigger than MOXIE," and must also liquefy the oxygen ([Hoffman, Hecht et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9432831/); [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/making-oxygen-on-mars)). NASA says ascent propellant is "the single largest category of mass" that must be delivered to the Martian surface ([NASA ACR 2024 white paper](https://www.lpi.usra.edu/lunar/strategies/resources/M2M_ACR2024_MarsAscentPropellantConsiderations.pdf)). By my estimate, a four-person, 500-day stay would breathe about 1.7 t of oxygen, roughly a fifteenth to a twentieth of the ascent load. Life-support oxygen comes almost free with a propellant plant.

NASA's Design Reference Architecture 5.0 shows what a real plant looks like: **two ~566 kg solid-oxide CO₂ electrolysis units drawing ~23.7 kWe each**, running continuously for **at least 300 days** on a **30 kWe, 7.8 t fission reactor**. The plant would also produce water and N₂/Ar buffer gas for breathing air, with methane shipped from Earth ([NASA DRA 5.0, SP-2009-566](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/373665main_nasa-sp-2009-566.pdf)). The 2009 trade study found that Martian oxygen for ascent gave "the best balance between mass savings, total volume required, and power generation needs." It predated the lessons of the 2018 global dust storm ([NASA ACR 2024](https://www.lpi.usra.edu/lunar/strategies/resources/M2M_ACR2024_MarsAscentPropellantConsiderations.pdf)).

### Methane fuel ties Jezero's survival to its water ore

Making methane on Mars as well brings the problem back to water, which is a disadvantage at Jezero. The Sabatier reaction (CO₂ + 4H₂ → CH₄ + 2H₂O) paired with water electrolysis nets out to **CO₂ + 2H₂O → CH₄ + 2O₂**. By my stoichiometry, every tonne of methane consumes **2.25 t of water and 2.75 t of CO₂ and co-produces 4 t of oxygen**. Hoffman's 9 t of methane would therefore need about **20 t of water**, which is more than M-WIP's entire 16 t reference demand. NASA's "kiloton-class" ISRU concept uses exactly this route: solid-oxide co-electrolysis of CO₂ and water, Sabatier methanation, and recycling of hydrogen through separation membranes ([NASA NTRS 20230017069](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230017069/downloads/SciTech%20Mars%20kiloton%20ISRU%20Final.pdf)). NASA's 2024 Strategic Analysis Cycle weighed three water sources for ascent propellant: water shipped from Earth, buried ice sheets, and regolith. It warned that the most ISRU-intensive options might require infrastructure to be set up "years before" a crew arrives ([NASA ACR 2024](https://www.lpi.usra.edu/lunar/strategies/resources/M2M_ACR2024_MarsAscentPropellantConsiderations.pdf)). For Jezero, a realistic plan is local oxygen from the air from day one, with methane either imported (DRA 5.0 style) or cooked from the sulfate stack at M-WIP's ~22 kW whole-plant scale. A nearby ice field is not an option. The alternative with the lowest risk, shipping hydrogen, gives about 20 t of propellant per tonne of hydrogen landed by my arithmetic, but liquid hydrogen boils away in storage.

## Radiation, thin air and dust force a buried, nuclear-powered base

Water and oxygen keep a crew alive only if the habitat also protects them from the environment. Curiosity's RAD instrument measured **0.64 ± 0.12 mSv/day on the surface and 1.84 mSv/day in cruise** ([DLR / Reitz](https://elib.dlr.de/106090/1/ME-SBA-2016-Reitz_MSL-RAD_ICES_2016.pdf)). A mission of 180 days out, 500 days on the surface and 180 days back accumulates about **1.01 Sv** ([Space.com on Hassler et al. 2014](https://www.space.com/23875-mars-radiation-life-manned-mission.html)). NASA's 2022 career limit is **600 mSv** for all ages and sexes ([NASA OCHMO](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/radiation-protection-technical-brief-ochmo.pdf)). A NASA-authored 2025 analysis concedes that "long Mars missions may approach or exceed" it ([NTRS 20250006168](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250006168/downloads/ASCEND%20Simonsen%20draft%201.pdf)). About two-thirds of that dose comes during transit, so the surface habitat has to make up the difference. Thin shielding makes things worse: modeling checked against RAD data shows dose **peaks about 30 cm below the surface** because regolith creates secondary neutrons, and reducing the annual dose to 100 mSv takes **about 1–1.6 m of regolith** ([Space.com on Zhang et al. 2022](https://www.space.com/mars-radiation-protection-astronauts-underground)). Perseverance has no radiation detector, so Gale's numbers stand in for Jezero. No one has measured radiation at Jezero itself.

Perseverance's MEDA weather station makes Jezero one of the best-characterized places on Mars. On landing day it recorded **718 Pa (about 7 mbar)**, and air temperature fell to −25.6 °C within half an hour ([JPL](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-first-weather-report-from-jezero-crater-on-mars/)). Over the first 250 sols the average was about **−55 °C, with 50–60 °C day–night swings**, and the daily pressure swing doubled during a regional dust storm ([Rodríguez-Manfredi et al., Nature Geoscience](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41561-022-01084-0)). At 0.7% of Earth's sea-level pressure, any breach is immediately life-threatening. Gravity is **0.38 g** ([NASA Mars Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html)), and its long-term effect on bone and muscle has never been measured in humans. NASA's Human Research Program lists gravity alongside radiation, isolation, distance and hostile environments as the five core hazards ([NASA HRP](https://www.nasa.gov/feature/step-2-station-space-laboratory-as-testbed-for-moon-mars)).

These conditions decide the power choice. Sunlight at Mars is **586.2 W/m², 43% of Earth's** ([NASA Mars Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html)). A mid-latitude site is lit for only about half of each sol, and dust storms persist "for weeks or months." In December 2024 NASA **baselined nuclear fission as the primary surface power for crewed Mars missions**, citing the need for at least **10 kW** even for a two-person, 30-day sortie and "hundreds of kW to MW-class" for propellant production ([NASA ACR24 Mars Surface Power Decision](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/acr24-mars-surface-power-decision.pdf)). DRA 5.0 had already found that a 25 kWe solar alternative would weigh **~22.5 t and cover 4,300 m²**, nearly three times the mass of the reactor ([NASA DRA 5.0](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/373665main_nasa-sp-2009-566.pdf)). The 2018 storm that ended the solar-powered Opportunity rover shows what is at stake ([Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2018/06/13/huge-dust-storm-knocks-aging-mars-rover-out-of-contact-with-earth/)). Perseverance's own plutonium power source gives only **about 110 W** ([JPL Mars 2020 press kit](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/press_kits/mars_2020/launch/mission/spacecraft/power/)), about 1% of a minimal crew's needs. The reactor that could fill the gap is still a lunar project. A 2025 directive set a **100 kWe, under-15-t reactor** for launch readiness by late 2029 ([ANS Nuclear Newswire](https://www.ans.org/news/2025-09-02/article-7336/nuclear-power-on-the-moon-what-were-watching/)), and NASA and DOE signed an MOU in January 2026 aiming for 2030 ([World Nuclear News](https://world-nuclear-news.org/articles/nasa-doe-join-together-for-lunar-reactor-by-2030)).

Shelter concepts that NASA has tested fit Jezero's geology. NASA's 3D-Printed Habitat Challenge awarded **$500,000** to AI SpaceFactory's MARSHA, a basalt-composite pod printed in about 30 hours that proved "stronger and more durable than its concrete competitors" ([CompositesWorld](https://compositesworld.com/news/basalt-fiber-composite-design-wins-nasa-3d-printed-habitat-challenge)). Jezero's olivine-rich floor and the Syrtis Major volcanics in its Exploration Zone are basaltic feedstock of that kind. Linking the two is my inference, not a NASA statement. NASA Langley's Mars Ice Home wraps an inflatable habitat in a water-ice shell that blocks cosmic rays, lets some light through, and could later be converted into ascent propellant ([Universe Today](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/nasa-might-build-ice-house-mars)). At Jezero, every tonne of that shell would have to be baked out of rock. ICON's 3D-printed **1,700 sq ft Mars Dune Alpha** at Johnson Space Center is where crews currently live out simulated Mars missions. CHAPEA Mission 2 began on 19 October 2025 and ends on **31 October 2026**, five days after the date of this report ([NASA CHAPEA](https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/chapea/)).

## Food stays mostly imported while crops add freshness

Food is the one survival need that Mars's own resources cannot meet any time soon. NASA plans around **3,000 kcal per crewmember per day, or 1.83 kg of packaged food**, and estimates that a six-person, 1,095-day Mars mission needs about **12 t** of food that stays edible for five years or more ([NASA NTRS 20140010665](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20140010665/downloads/20140010665.pdf); [NTRS 20110016041](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20110016041/downloads/20110016041.pdf)). Growing food has been demonstrated only in protected conditions. Veggie and the Advanced Plant Habitat on the ISS have grown lettuce, cabbage, kale and **Hatch chile peppers from seed** ([CNN via ABC17](https://abc17news.com/news/national-world/cnn-national/2021/07/18/astronauts-on-international-space-station-are-growing-chile-peppers-in-a-first-for-nasa/)). Nine of ten crops produced edible harvests in NASA's JSC Mars-1A simulant, but in an Earth greenhouse, under Earth air, and in a simulant that **contains no perchlorate** ([Sci.News on Wamelink et al. 2019](https://www.sci.news/space/crops-simulated-martian-lunar-dirt-07697.html)). Interstellar Lab won NASA's 2024 Deep Space Food Challenge with **$750,000** for a nine-module system producing microgreens, vegetables, mushrooms and insects ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-awards-1-25-million-to-three-teams-at-deep-space-food-finale)). The challenge aimed to supplement stored food, not replace it. A believable Jezero farm would be a hydroponic module under artificial light, drawing on the reactor and the water plant. It would not use raw crater soil, whose chlorine content would first have to be leached out.

## What a walkable Jezero map should actually show

Putting the evidence together gives a clear spatial story for "Martian Story." Jezero was chosen for Mars 2020 in November 2018, out of more than 60 candidates, because its **3.6-billion-year-old delta** preserves clays and carbonates ([JPL](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasa-announces-landing-site-for-mars-2020-rover/)). It was also formally proposed as a **human Exploration Zone** at NASA's 2015 landing-site workshop. That proposal was centered at **17.747°N, 77.037°E** and listed delta sediments, carbonate- and serpentine-altered olivine, clay-rich basement, the 500 m Mg-sulfate stack and Syrtis volcanics as regions of interest ([Mustard et al., abstract 1034](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/explorationzone2015/pdf/1034.pdf); [NASA NTRS 20160002370](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160002370/downloads/20160002370.pdf)). Each survival system therefore corresponds to a place a visitor can walk to.

| Survival need | Jezero source (NASA evidence) | Status | Map anchor |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oxygen | CO₂ air via MOXIE-type electrolysis | **Demonstrated at Jezero** (122 g) | Perseverance landing area |
| Water | Hydrated Mg/Fe sulfates, clays, 1.5–3 wt% regolith | Projected; Jezero hydration levels unmeasured | Western fan front mudstones; Mg-sulfate stack in the Exploration Zone |
| Methane fuel | Sabatier using water from minerals + CO₂ | Projected; ~2.25 t water per t CH₄ | Same as water |
| Radiation shelter | 1–1.6 m regolith berm or 3D-printed basalt | Modeled / Earth-tested | Crater floor (Séitah's solid mafic rock) |
| Power | Fission reactor (NASA 2024 baseline) | Lunar prototype targeted ~2030 | Site set apart from the habitat |
| Food | Imported plus hydroponics | ISS-demonstrated | Inside the habitat |
| Hazard | Perchlorate-rich soil (Cl to 2 wt%), dust storms | Measured | Fan-front regolith |

Several facts cannot be shown accurately because nobody has measured them. These include a SWIM ice-consistency value for the Isidis/Jezero region, the water content of Jezero's sulfates, surface radiation at Jezero, and any NASA decision on whether the Jezero region suits a crewed landing. A 2025 *Planetary Science Journal* overlay study ranked the Jezero Exploration Zone highly among the 2015 proposals ([PSJ](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/ade30e)), but NASA has not made a site decision. The map should label these as open questions rather than settled facts.

## Conclusion

The NASA record changes the usual picture of Mars survival. Oxygen, often seen as the hardest problem, is the only resource actually produced on Mars, and it was produced at Jezero. Water, usually assumed to be easy, is the real constraint at low latitudes. Jezero shows the dilemma clearly: the site that is best for warmth, landing and science is roughly 700–1,000 km from usable ice. A crew there would trade shovels for kilns and would depend on the reactor that NASA chose in 2024 but has not yet built, even for the Moon. A useful finding for the project is that M-WIP's preferred mineral ore, poly-hydrated sulfate, is exactly what the 2015 Jezero Exploration Zone proposal highlighted. That makes the sulfate-bearing fan front and the Mg-sulfate stack the site's most important survival locations, more than any ice deposit.

Going forward, the gaps that matter most for Jezero are not engineering problems but measurements. Nobody has measured how much water Jezero's sulfates and clays actually hold, how far perchlorate stays behind during evaporation, or how much radiation reaches the crater floor. Each of these would turn a projected survival path into a demonstrated one. Until those measurements exist, "Martian Story" is most faithful to NASA data if it treats Jezero as a place where survival is engineered from rock, air and fission, and where every litre of water and every kilogram of methane has to be mined, heated and cleaned.
