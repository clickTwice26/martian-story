# Mars Survival Factors Beyond Water and Oxygen: Radiation, Environment, Food, Shelter, Power, and Jezero Suitability (NASA-centric data, current to Oct 2026)

Notation: **[M]** = measured by an instrument or experiment; **[E]** = estimate, model, or projection; **[P]** = policy, standard, or program fact.
Research note: Firecrawl hit rate limits and WebFetch could not resolve nasa.gov during this session. Several numbers below therefore come from search-result summaries of the cited page rather than a full read of it, and they are flagged where that matters.

## 1. Radiation: How much dose would a Mars crew get, what limit applies, and how can they shield?

### Takeaway
Curiosity's RAD instrument measured about 0.64–0.67 mSv/day on the Martian surface and about 1.8 mSv/day in cruise. A reference mission of 180 days out, 500 days on the surface, and 180 days back adds up to about 1.0 Sv [E]. That exceeds NASA's 600 mSv career limit, adopted in 2022 [P], so a long-stay crew has to rely on shielding. Regolith about 1–1.6 m thick, lava tubes, or water/ice walls are the main options; a habitat that relied on its walls alone would not be enough.

### Cited Findings
**Measured doses (Curiosity RAD)**
- [M] Average GCR dose-equivalent rate on the Martian surface (Gale Crater) was **0.67 mSv/day** from Aug 2012 to Jun 2013 (Hassler et al. 2014, *Science*). — [SwRI press release](https://www.swri.org/newsroom/press-releases/swri-scientists-publish-first-radiation-measurements-the-surface-of-mars)
- [M] A DLR analysis of the same instrument gives **0.64 ± 0.12 mSv/day** on the surface (7 Aug 2012 – 1 Jun 2013) and **1.84 ± 0.3 mSv/day** during cruise. — [DLR / Reitz, ICES 2016 PDF](https://elib.dlr.de/106090/1/ME-SBA-2016-Reitz_MSL-RAD_ICES_2016.pdf)
- [M] Surface GCR absorbed-dose rate ranged from **180 to 225 µGy/day**. — [Science in the Classroom, Hassler et al. 2014 annotated](https://www.scienceintheclassroom.org/research-papers/curiosity-tells-all-about-mars-radiation-environment)
- [M] Press summary: a person on the surface would receive about **0.7 mSv/day**, varying between about 0.4 and 1.0 mSv/day. — [SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/curiosity-finds-fairly-benign-radiation-environment-on-mars/)
- [M] Zeitlin et al. 2013 (*Science*) found that RAD received about **466 ± 84 mSv** over the 253-day cruise, an average of about **1.8 mSv/day**. — [Science Chronicle summary](https://sciencechronicle.in/2016/01/12/radiation-risk-to-astronauts-travelling-to-mars-measured); [SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/?p=489264). I could not access the primary paper.
- [E] Zeitlin et al. 2013: a round trip with 180 days each way and similar shielding gives about **662 ± 108 mSv** for transit alone. The MSL spacecraft's shielding is described as "roughly similar" to future crewed vehicles. — [Science Chronicle](https://sciencechronicle.in/2016/01/12/radiation-risk-to-astronauts-travelling-to-mars-measured); [SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/?p=489264)
- [E] Hassler et al. 2014: a mission of 180-day cruise, 500-day surface stay, and 180-day return (860 days total) gives about **1.01 Sv**, roughly 10 times a 6-month ISS mission. The measurements were taken near solar maximum. — [Space.com](https://www.space.com/23875-mars-radiation-life-manned-mission.html); [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/sciencemain/radiation-mars-manageable-manned-mission-curiosity-finds-2D11716674); [CSMonitor](https://www.csmonitor.com/Science/2013/1209/Curiosity-measures-radiation-at-Martian-surface)

**NASA limits**
- [P] NASA-STD-3001 (2022): career effective dose from spaceflight radiation must be **< 600 mSv**, "universal for all ages and sexes." A separate limit caps each solar particle event at **250 mSv** effective dose. — [NASA OCHMO Radiation Protection Technical Brief (PDF)](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/radiation-protection-technical-brief-ochmo.pdf)
- [P] The 600 mSv limit replaced the older 3% risk-of-exposure-induced-death (REID) rule, which varied by age and sex. The National Academies (2021) endorsed it as an approximation within ±10%. — [National Academies](https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/nasa-should-update-astronaut-radiation-exposure-limits-improve-communication-of-cancer-risks)
- [E] A NASA-authored 2025 paper: the new limit "extends permissible mission durations by a factor of ~1.5 to >3" compared with the old limit, but "long Mars missions may approach or exceed" it. Cardiovascular and cognitive risks remain uncertain. — [NTRS, Simonsen ASCEND 2025 draft (PDF)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250006168/downloads/ASCEND%20Simonsen%20draft%201.pdf)
- [E] For scale, 600 mSv equals almost 4 years on the ISS. — [BBC Sky at Night](https://www.skyatnightmagazine.com/news/new-radiation-limits-for-astronauts-could-make-spaceflight-farer/)

**Shielding**
- [E] Regolith (Zhang et al. 2022, *JGR Planets*, AtRIS model checked against RAD data): neutron flux and effective dose **peak about 30 cm below the surface**, because the regolith generates secondary particles. Bringing the annual effective dose down to **100 mSv** requires **about 1 to 1.6 m** of regolith. More shielding lowers the heavy-ion contribution but raises the neutron share. — [Space.com summary](https://www.space.com/mars-radiation-protection-astronauts-underground); [Göttingen e-docs record](https://e-docs.geo-leo.de/handle/11858/9345)
- [E] NASA Langley (Simonsen et al. 1990) modeled GCR and solar-flare proton transport through the atmosphere and regolith for a habitat at 0 km and 8 km altitude. It shows dose at the habitat center falling as shield thickness increases. I could not extract the specific values. — [NTRS 19900062325](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19900062325)
- [E] A Bristol study estimated that 1 m of regolith cuts the *primary* particle dose by about 41% for a 30-day stay. That figure leaves out secondary neutrons. — [Univ. of Bristol](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/8864a0bf-ae44-450b-9f37-4f733dd0dfc8)
- [E] Lava tubes (Paris et al. 2023, not a NASA study, though it used NASA orbiter imagery): radiation measured inside Earth lava tubes was extrapolated to Mars. Tubes might block a further ~82% of incoming radiation, and low-lying Hellas receives ~50% less than higher regions. One skylight on Pavonis Mons is 180 m wide. — [Space.com](https://www.space.com/mars-lava-tubes-radiation-safe.html); [IFLScience](https://www.iflscience.com/researchers-identify-underground-lava-tubes-on-mars-that-could-shelter-first-explorers-56012)
- [E] Water and ice walls: NASA Langley's Mars Ice Home concept (2017) surrounds an inflatable inner-tube habitat with a water-ice shell to block GCR while letting some light through (see Section 4). — [Universe Today](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/nasa-might-build-ice-house-mars)

### Inferences
- Arithmetic check: 180 d × 1.8 mSv/d × 2 ≈ 650 mSv, plus 500 d × 0.64 mSv/d ≈ 320 mSv, for a total of about 970 mSv. That is consistent with the ~1.01 Sv estimate and about 1.6–1.7 times the 600 mSv career limit. Transit accounts for about two-thirds of the dose.
- The Martian surface is about half as harsh as deep space. This is because the planet blocks roughly half the sky, and the thin atmosphere (about 20 g/cm² column) adds a little more protection.
- For a story set at Jezero (about –2.6 km elevation, per general knowledge; not verified here), surface dose would be similar to Gale's (about –4.5 km). Gale's lower elevation means more atmosphere overhead, so Jezero may be slightly higher. That last point is my inference.
- A buried habitat needs well over 30 cm of cover. A thin layer makes the dose *worse* because of the neutron peak; about 1–1.6 m or more is the target.

### Gaps
- I could not open the primary *Science* papers (Hassler 2014, Zeitlin 2013), so the figures come from press summaries and the DLR paper.
- I found no NASA-authored, peer-reviewed estimate of dose inside a Martian lava tube. The figure of "~62 µSv/day in a Hellas tube" appeared only on an aggregator and is unverified.
- I found no RAD-equivalent radiation measurements at Jezero itself, since Perseverance carries no RAD.

## 2. Environment: What are temperature, pressure, dust, UV, toxic soil, and gravity like (with Jezero specifics)?

### Takeaway
At Jezero, Perseverance's MEDA measured an average air temperature of about –55 °C with day–night swings of 50–60 °C, and pressure of about 7 mbar (718 Pa on landing day). That is less than 1% of Earth's sea-level pressure and far below the pressure at which body fluids boil, so pressure suits or pressurized habitats are mandatory. The 2018 global dust storm killed the solar-powered Opportunity rover. Soil perchlorate (about 0.5–1 wt%) is a thyroid toxin. Gravity is 0.38 g, with health effects that are still poorly quantified.

### Cited Findings
**Global baselines (NASA Mars Fact Sheet)**
- [M] Mean surface pressure is **6.36 mb**, varying from **4.0 to 8.7 mb** with season. The Viking 1 site read 6.9–9 mb. — [NASA NSSDC Mars Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html)
- [M] Average temperature is about **214 K (–59 °C)**. At the Viking 1 site, temperatures ran from **184 to 242 K (–89 to –31 °C)** over a day. — [NASA NSSDC Mars Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html)
- [M] The atmosphere is **95.1% CO₂, 2.59% N₂, 1.94% Ar, 0.16% O₂**. A day lasts **24.66 h**. — [NASA NSSDC Mars Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html)
- [M] Mean surface gravity is **3.73 m/s² = 0.380 × Earth**. — [NASA NSSDC Mars Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html)

**Jezero (Perseverance MEDA)**
- [M] First readings (Feb 2021): air temperature started just below **–20 °C (–4 °F)** and fell to **–25.6 °C (–14 °F)** within 30 minutes. Pressure was **718 Pa**, inside the model prediction of 705–735 Pa. — [JPL: NASA's First Weather Report from Jezero](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-first-weather-report-from-jezero-crater-on-mars/)
- [M] First 250 sols (Rodríguez-Manfredi et al., *Nature Geoscience*): average air temperature was about **–55 °C**, with day–night swings of **50–60 °C**. Nights have a stable thermal inversion; daytime brings a highly turbulent convective regime with large vertical temperature gradients. These figures come from a search summary of the paper. — [Nature Geoscience s41561-022-01084-0](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41561-022-01084-0)
- [M] The daily pressure swing is **2–5% of the sol-average (10–35 Pa)** and tracks the amount of dust in the air. During a regional dust storm starting at Ls 135°, the daily swing roughly doubled. — [Nature Geoscience s41561-022-01084-0](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41561-022-01084-0); [EGU22-7047](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU22/EGU22-7047.html)
- [M] A 414-sol pressure dataset follows the expected seasonal CO₂ cycle and agrees with other landers. — [Research.fi dataset: MEDA pressure initial results](https://research.fi/en/results/dataset/03bfae7b-acf6-4fd7-889a-fa046cf17211)

**Dust storms**
- [M] The 2018 global dust storm began in late May 2018 (southern spring). Opportunity, 14 years past its 90-day design life, lost contact after shutting down everything but its clock. — [Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2018/06/13/huge-dust-storm-knocks-aging-mars-rover-out-of-contact-with-earth/)
- [M] The storm faded from August: optical depth (tau) was about 2.5 by 7 Aug 2018, and later a "normal" tau of about 0.8 marked its end. NASA ended the mission after more than 8 months of silence, a 15-year mission in all. The tau values come from secondary or forum reposts of NASA statements. — [The Weather Network](https://www.theweathernetwork.com/en/news/science/space/nasa-venerable-mars-opportunity-rover-mission-ends-after-contact-lost-dust-storm?b=81); [forum repost](https://forum.daffodilvarsity.edu.bd/index.php/topic,52906.msg137705.html)
- [P/E] NASA's 2024 architecture paper: dust storms range "from local dust devils to regional or global storms persisting for weeks or months," and a local storm can grow global "in just a few Martian days." Dust on solar arrays "can prevent them from generating keep-alive power (proving fatal to the Opportunity rover)." — [NASA ACR24 Mars Surface Power Decision (PDF)](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/acr24-mars-surface-power-decision.pdf)

**Perchlorate toxicity**
- [M] Phoenix (2008) found perchlorate in the soil. PI Peter Smith said "half a percent is a fair amount." A review by Davila, McKay et al. puts Martian soil perchlorate at **0.5–1%**. Curiosity later confirmed perchlorate at Gale. — [Space.com](https://space.com/21554-mars-toxic-perchlorate-chemicals.html); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/news-blog/perchlorate-found-on-mars-makes-soi-2008-08-04/)
- [E] Perchlorate interferes with thyroid iodide uptake and can cause hypothyroidism. McKay: "toxic for humans, as it interferes with the thyroid." Orbital chlorine maps suggest it is spread across the whole planet. Perchlorate is also a possible O₂ source. — [Space.com](https://space.com/21554-mars-toxic-perchlorate-chemicals.html)

**Gravity and other hazards**
- [P] NASA's Human Research Program groups risks into **five hazards**: radiation, isolation and confinement, distance from Earth, gravity fields, and hostile or closed environments. Crews would live in about 3/8 g for up to 2 years, which affects bones, muscles, and the cardiovascular system. — [NASA (HRP feature)](https://www.nasa.gov/feature/step-2-station-space-laboratory-as-testbed-for-moon-mars); [SlashGear summary](https://www.slashgear.com/983400/nasas-5-hazards-of-human-spaceflight-explained)
- [M] In *microgravity*, bone density falls by **more than 1% per month** without loading. No Mars-gravity rate has been measured. This comes from a secondary summary of NASA statements. — [Beebom summary of NASA HRP](https://beebom.com/nasa-hazards-human-spaceflight-mars/)

### Inferences
- Jezero's pressure (about 7 mbar) is about 0.7% of Earth's sea-level 1,013 mbar. Any breach or suit failure is immediately life-threatening.
- A daily swing of 50–60 °C means habitat thermal control must handle about –80 to –20 °C outside air in the same day. The ground surface swings even more. That last point is my inference.
- Jezero is at about 18°N. Its seasons are milder than the poles but colder than equatorial lowlands.
- Perchlorate means regolith dust tracked indoors is a chronic chemical hazard. It also matters for growing plants in regolith (see Section 3).

### Gaps
- UV: I found no sourced NASA numbers on surface UV flux at Jezero in this session (MEDA has a radiation and dust sensor that measures UV). The Armstrong limit (about 6.3 kPa / 63 mbar, from general knowledge) was not verified against a source this session.
- No measured human data exist for 0.38 g. The partial-gravity bone and muscle response is unknown.
- I did not find the official NASA/JPL press release giving the exact date Opportunity's mission was declared over (13 Feb 2019 from general knowledge; unverified here).
- I found no perchlorate concentration measured specifically at Jezero.

## 3. Food: How much does a crew need, and what has been shown about growing it?

### Takeaway
NASA plans around about **3,000 kcal per crew member per day**, which takes about 1.83 kg of packaged food. A 6-person, 1,095-day Mars mission would need about 12 t of food [E]. Crops have grown on the ISS (Veggie, APH), and nine of ten crops grew in a NASA Mars regolith simulant (Wamelink, under Earth atmosphere). NASA's Deep Space Food Challenge (won by Interstellar Lab, 2024) aimed at bioregenerative supplements to stored food, not replacements for it.

### Cited Findings
- [P/E] NASA Advanced Food Technology: "current mass requirement for **3000 kcal per crewmember per day is 1.83 kg**." Food volume is about **0.00472 m³ per crew member per day** including packaging. A **Mars scenario of 6 crew for 1,095 days needs about 12,023 kg** of food. — [NTRS 20140010665 (PDF)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20140010665/downloads/20140010665.pdf); [NTRS 20140006529 (PDF)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20140006529/downloads/20140006529.pdf)
- [E] Reducing water content saves **321 g per crew member per day**. A combined best case of denser, higher-fat foods could save up to **529 g per day (36%)**. Mars food must keep its quality for **5 years or longer**. — [NTRS 20110016041 (PDF)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20110016041/downloads/20110016041.pdf); [Space.com](https://www.space.com/12919-nasa-mars-astronaut-space-food.html)
- [M] ISS: NASA's Vegetable Production System (Veggie) has grown lettuce (including "Outredgeous" red romaine, Veg-03, 2016), cabbage, kale, and zinnias. The Advanced Plant Habitat (APH), the largest plant facility on the ISS with about **180 sensors**, grew **48 Hatch chile seeds** (PH-04, planted 12 Jul 2021), the first NASA crop of chiles grown from seed. — [CNN via ABC17](https://abc17news.com/news/national-world/cnn-national/2021/07/18/astronauts-on-international-space-station-are-growing-chile-peppers-in-a-first-for-nasa/); [Fox (NASA pepper harvest)](https://www.fox10phoenix.com/news/nasa-astronauts-pick-peppers-in-space-for-the-first-time-on-international-space-station)
- [M] Wamelink et al. (Wageningen; *Open Agriculture* 2019, doi 10.1515/opag-2019-0051) grew 10 crops in NASA's **JSC Mars-1A** simulant, with potting soil as a control: garden cress, rocket, tomato, radish, rye, quinoa, spinach, chives, peas, and leek. **Nine of the ten** gave edible harvests; spinach failed. Biomass on the Mars simulant was slightly lower than the control. The trials ran in an Earth greenhouse under Earth air. JSC-1A contains **no perchlorate**. — [Sci.News](https://www.sci.news/space/crops-simulated-martian-lunar-dirt-07697.html); [ScienceAlert](https://sciencealert.com/tomatoes-peas-and-8-other-crops-have-been-grown-in-mars-equivalent-soil)
- [M] Wamelink team intercropping study (*PLOS ONE*, 2024): tomatoes grown alongside peas and carrots in Mars simulant produced about **twice the yield** of tomatoes grown alone. Peas and carrots did not benefit. — [VOA Learning English](https://learningenglish.voanews.com/a/scientists-explore-how-to-grow-crops-on-mars/7595327.html)
- [P/M] NASA Deep Space Food Challenge finale (Aug 2024): **Interstellar Lab** (Merritt Island, FL) won the **$750,000** U.S. grand prize; the three U.S. finalists shared **$1.25M** in total. Its NUCLEUS system has **9 modules** (6 for food, 3 for support) and produces microgreens, vegetables, mushrooms, and insects. — [NASA release](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-awards-1-25-million-to-three-teams-at-deep-space-food-finale); [Space.com](https://www.space.com/nasa-deep-space-food-challenge-winners-2024)
- [P] CHAPEA Mission 1 (2023–24) included crop growing as part of its resource-limited simulated Mars life (see Section 4). — [NASA CHAPEA](https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/chapea/)

### Inferences
- Hydroponic or aeroponic growing inside pressurized, lit, heated modules is the realistic near-term path (Veggie/APH/NUCLEUS). Raw regolith growing would require removing perchlorate first, for example by leaching with water or with perchlorate-reducing bacteria. That removal step is my inference; I found no NASA source on it.
- The ~12 t food mass for a 3-year, 6-person mission is a strong driver for supplementing with crops. Crops currently provide freshness, micronutrients, and morale rather than bulk calories.
- Under Mars sunlight (about 43% of Earth's at the top of the atmosphere, less through dust), greenhouses would need artificial lighting or concentrators. That would compete for power (see Section 5).

### Gaps
- I found no NASA source that gives an updated official kcal standard (the 3,000 kcal figure is from 2011–2014 NASA documents) or that describes a validated perchlorate-removal process for growing food.
- I found no plant-growth test in perchlorate-spiked simulant under Mars-like pressure in this session.
- I did not check the ISS chile harvest outcome or recent APH/Veggie results (2023–2026).

## 4. Shelter and building materials: What has NASA demonstrated or studied?

### Takeaway
NASA's 3D-Printed Habitat Challenge (2015–2019) showed that robotically printed regolith composites can work. AI SpaceFactory's basalt/PLA "MARSHA" won $500k and beat concrete in strength tests. ICON's 3D-printed 1,700 sq ft Mars Dune Alpha at JSC hosts the year-long CHAPEA analog missions. Mission 1 ran June 2023 – July 2024; Mission 2 began 19 Oct 2025 and runs to 31 Oct 2026. NASA Langley's Mars Ice Home is an inflatable habitat with a water-ice radiation shell.

### Cited Findings
- [P/M] NASA Centennial Challenge **3D-Printed Habitat Challenge** (began 2015; three phases: design, materials, construction). **AI SpaceFactory** won **$500,000** in 2019 for MARSHA, a vertical pod about **15 ft (4.6 m)** tall printed in about **30 h**, with 3 robotically placed windows. **Penn State** took second place and **$200,000**. — [WEF](https://weforum.org/agenda/2019/05/nasa-held-a-contest-to-design-a-3d-habitat-on-mars-and-the-winner-is-a-vertical-pod-that-can-be-printed-in-30-hours); [CompositesWorld](https://compositesworld.com/news/basalt-fiber-composite-design-wins-nasa-3d-printed-habitat-challenge)
- [M] The MARSHA material is a basalt-fiber or crushed-basalt-regolith composite with plant-derived PLA bioplastic. Descriptions differ between sources. It passed NASA pressure, smoke, and impact tests and was "stronger and more durable than its concrete competitors." — [CompositesWorld](https://compositesworld.com/news/basalt-fiber-composite-design-wins-nasa-3d-printed-habitat-challenge); [Autodesk](https://www.autodesk.com/autodesk-university/article/Autonomous-Construction-Sustainability-Lessons-NASA-3D-Printed-Habitat-Challenge-2020)
- [P] **CHAPEA** (Crew Health and Performance Exploration Analog) takes place in **Mars Dune Alpha**, a **1,700 sq ft (about 158 m²)** habitat **3D-printed by ICON** at NASA Johnson. — [NASA CHAPEA](https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/chapea/)
- [M] **CHAPEA Mission 1** ran **25 Jun 2023 – 6 Jul 2024** (378 days). Crew: Kelly Haston, Ross Brockwell, Nathan Jones, Anca Selariu. It simulated communication delays of up to **22 min one way**. — [NASA CHAPEA](https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/chapea/); [ABC News](https://abcnews.com/US/nasas-mars-simulator-crew-emerged-after-378-days/story?id=111755446)
- [M/P] **CHAPEA Mission 2** runs **19 Oct 2025 – 31 Oct 2026** (378 days). Crew: Ross Elder (commander), Ellen Ellis (medical officer), Matthew Montgomery (science officer), James Spicer. NASA marked "one year inside habitat" in Oct 2026. It studies physical and behavioral health and performance under isolation and limited resources, with simulated EVAs and a simulated 2-week communication blackout. — [NASA CHAPEA (updated 22 Oct 2026)](https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/chapea/); [NASA JSC](https://www.facebook.com/NASAJSC/videos/theyre-not-just-simulating-mars-theyre-living-it-the-chapea-mission-2-crew-enter/1139840728300685/)
- [P/E] **NASA Langley Mars Ice Home** (2017, Center Innovation Fund, with SEArch and Clouds AO): an inflatable, inner-tube-shaped habitat surrounded by a **water-ice shell**. Simple robots would deploy it and fill it with water before the crew arrives. The ice blocks GCR while transmitting some light. The water could later be turned into propellant for the Mars Ascent Vehicle. NASA called it one concept among many; long-term material durability is open. — [Universe Today](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/nasa-might-build-ice-house-mars); [ScienceAlert](https://www.sciencealert.com/nasa-might-build-ice-homes-on-mars); [CSMonitor](https://www.csmonitor.com/Science/2017/0102/NASA-s-latest-plan-for-housing-astronauts-on-Mars-ice-homes)
- [E] Lava tubes as natural shelter: skylights could be sealed and the cave pressurized. Tubes also offer protection from micrometeorites and buffered temperatures (see Section 1). — [Space.com](https://www.space.com/mars-lava-tubes-radiation-safe.html)

### Inferences
- A story-plausible Jezero base: a landed or inflatable core, covered with at least 1–1.6 m of bagged or 3D-printed regolith or a water/ice jacket for radiation (Section 1). Jezero's olivine/basalt and the Syrtis Major volcanic rocks would provide basaltic feedstock similar to the MARSHA material. Linking Jezero's geology to MARSHA feedstock is my inference.
- Sulfur concrete is attractive because Jezero-region sulfates exist (the Mg-sulfate stack in the Mustard EZ proposal, Section 6). However, I found no NASA source this session that quantifies sulfur concrete.

### Gaps
- I found no sourced NASA data this session on sulfur concrete, on inflatable habitats such as TransHab, Sierra Space LIFE, or BEAM (for example their pressure ratings), or on ICON's NASA Olympus / Moon-to-Mars construction contract.
- No CHAPEA Mission 3 plans appear on the NASA page as of Oct 2026, and Mission 1 science results were not retrieved.

## 5. Power: What power is available at Mars, and what has NASA chosen?

### Takeaway
Sunlight at Mars is 586.2 W/m² at the top of the atmosphere, 43% of Earth's 1,361 W/m². Dust storms lasting weeks to months and dust settling on panels make solar risky; the 2018 storm killed Opportunity. Perseverance runs on an MMRTG giving about 110 W. In 2024 NASA chose **nuclear fission as the primary surface power for crewed Mars missions**, needing at least 10 kW for a minimal 2-person, 30-day stay and hundreds of kW to MW for propellant production. The lunar Fission Surface Power program was sped up in 2025 to a 100 kWe reactor by about 2030 [P].

### Cited Findings
- [M] Solar irradiance at Mars is **586.2 W/m²**, against **1,361.0 W/m²** for Earth (**ratio 0.431**). — [NASA NSSDC Mars Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html)
- [P/E] **NASA 2024 Architecture Concept Review decision**: NASA "baselined nuclear fission power as the primary surface power generation technology" for initial crewed Mars missions, over photovoltaic arrays with storage. The reasons given: round-the-clock output, survival through global dust storms, and lower landed mass and volume. Key figures from the document: Mars solar flux is "at most 45 percent" of Earth's. A mid-latitude site is lit for only about **50%** of the about 25-hour sol. A minimal mission (**2 crew, ≤30 days, with a pressurized rover**) needs **at least 10 kW**. Larger crews, longer stays, and propellant ISRU need **hundreds of kW to MW-class** power. Removing dust from panels does not fix the loss of sunlight during long storms. — [NASA ACR24 Mars Surface Power Decision (PDF)](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/acr24-mars-surface-power-decision.pdf)
- [M/P] **Perseverance MMRTG** produces "about **110 watts**" at the start of the mission, declining a few percent a year. It carries **4.8 kg (10.6 lb)** of plutonium dioxide (Pu-238). Two Li-ion batteries cover peak demand of up to **900 W**. About 95% of its output is heat, which is used to warm the rover. Its design life is given as **14 years** (POWER) or **17 years** (INL); the sources conflict. — [JPL Mars 2020 press kit: Power](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/press_kits/mars_2020/launch/mission/spacecraft/power/); [INL Mars 2020](https://inl.gov/mars-2020/); [POWER Magazine](https://www.powermag.com/the-nuclear-battery-aboard-perseverance-the-next-gen-mars-rover/)
- [M] Older NASA studies: surface solar varies with Mars–Sun distance, solar zenith angle, and dust. "Appreciable diffuse sunlight" remains even at high opacity. Northern spring and summer give **2–5 kWh/m²/day**. Dust settling on MER arrays degraded their output. — [NTRS 19920024655](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19920024655); [NTRS 20050196566](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20050196566); [NTRS 19950004977 (PDF)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19950004977/downloads/19950004977.pdf)
- [P] **Fission Surface Power (lunar)**: Acting Administrator Sean Duffy signed a directive (dated 31 Jul or 4 Aug 2025; sources differ) requiring a reactor of **≥100 kWe** with mass **< 15 t** and closed-Brayton-cycle power conversion. It must be ready to launch by **Q1 FY2030** (late calendar 2029). Two industry awards were planned, with a possible down-select to one. Steven Sinacore was named program executive. — [ANS Nuclear Newswire](https://www.ans.org/news/2025-09-02/article-7336/nuclear-power-on-the-moon-what-were-watching/); [NEI Magazine](https://www.neimagazine.com/news/nasa-fast-tracks-lunar-reactor-plan/); [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/08/15/nuclear_moon/)
- [P] In Jan 2026, NASA and DOE signed an MOU on a lunar surface fission reactor. A 18 Dec 2025 executive order calls for initial lunar outpost elements by 2030 as a step toward Mars. Whether a reactor will be *operating* by 2030 is unclear, and the program has a history of delays. — [ANS (14 Jan 2026)](https://www.ans.org/news/2026-01-14/article-7674/nasa-doe-solidify-collaboration-on-a-lunar-surface-reactor/)

### Inferences
- At Jezero (about 18°N), a photovoltaic farm sized for about 10 kW of continuous power would need large overbuild plus storage. NASA's own analysis argues for fission. A story base would plausibly run on one or more ~40–100 kWe reactors (the lunar FSP design adapted for Mars), with solar as backup.
- A Perseverance-class MMRTG (about 110 W) is about 1% of the minimum crew-mission need. RTGs suit rovers and emergency power, not habitats.

## 6. Jezero suitability: Why did NASA pick Jezero, and was it proposed as a human Exploration Zone?

### Takeaway
NASA picked Jezero for Mars 2020 in Nov 2018 after a 5-year search of more than 60 sites. The draw was its 3.6-billion-year-old lake-delta system with clays and carbonates that are good at preserving biosignatures. Jezero *was* proposed as a human landing Exploration Zone at NASA's Oct 27–30, 2015 First Landing Site/Exploration Zone Workshop (abstract #1034, Mustard et al.). That proposal centered on 17.75°N, 77.04°E and noted carbonates, serpentine, phyllosilicates (clay minerals), and a 500 m Mg-sulfate stack. The abstract summary did not mention water ice.

### Cited Findings
- [P] NASA selected **Jezero** on **19 Nov 2018** after a **5-year search** of **more than 60 candidate sites**. The lake-delta has "at least five kinds of rock, including clays and carbonates," which have high potential to preserve signs of past life. The landforms date back to about **3.6 billion years**. Hazards include boulders to the east, cliffs to the west, and dune-filled depressions. — [JPL / NASA release](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasa-announces-landing-site-for-mars-2020-rover/); [Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2018/11/19/nasa-picks-jezero-crater-landing-site-for-next-mars-rover/)
- [P] The final three Mars 2020 candidates were **Jezero, Columbia Hills, and Northeast Syrtis** (2017 workshop). NE Syrtis lies about 30 km or more southwest of Jezero. — [Brown University (2015)](https://news.brown.edu/articles/2015/08/mars2020); [NTRS Chojnacki et al. 2018](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003827/downloads/Chojnacki_et_al-2018-JGR_Wind_Driven_Erosin.pdf?attachment=true)
- [P] NASA's **First Landing Site/Exploration Zone Workshop for Human Missions to the Surface of Mars** was held **27–30 Oct 2015** in Houston. **47** EZ and ROI proposals were presented, covering all longitudes and latitudes within ±50°. An **Exploration Zone is a set of Regions of Interest within about 100 km of a central landing site**. ROIs cover both science and resources or capabilities for sustained human presence. — [LPI Contribution 1879 (program)](https://www.lpi.usra.edu/lpi/contribution_docs/LPI-001879.pdf); [NTRS 20160002370 (PDF)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160002370/downloads/20160002370.pdf); [NTRS 20150013826](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20150013826)
- [P] Follow-on: the joint HEOMD/SMD **Human Landing Sites Study (HLS2)** used MRO to image candidate EZs, with up to **3 MRO targets per EZ**. — [NASA HLS2 overview (PDF)](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/hls2-overview-v3tagged.pdf); [NTRS 20160001040](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20160001040)
- [M/E] **Abstract #1034: "Jezero Crater Watershed, Isidis Basin, Sulfate Deposits and Syrtis Major: A Compelling Exploration Zone for Human Exploration"** by J. F. Mustard, T. A. Goudge, M. S. Bramble, B. L. Ehlmann, J. W. Head, J. L. Dickson, C. I. Fassett, and K. M. Cannon. Landing site **17.747°N, 77.037°E**. Regions of interest: delta sedimentary rocks with well-preserved bottomset beds suited to preserving organics; extensive ancient **olivine altered to carbonate and serpentine**; pre-Isidis basement rocks rich in **water-bearing phyllosilicates**; a **500 m thick stack of hydrous Mg-sulfate-bearing layered strata**; and Hesperian Syrtis Major volcanics plus Noachian Isidis basin material. — [LPI abstract 1034 (PDF)](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/explorationzone2015/pdf/1034.pdf)
- [E] A 2025 *Planetary Science Journal* multi-criteria overlay analysis of the 2015 EZ proposals lists the Jezero crater EZ (#1034) with a score of 90. Other entries include Firsoff crater (89) and Gale (#1020) among the rankings. I saw only a snippet, so the score's meaning is unverified. — [PSJ 10.3847/PSJ/ade30e](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/ade30e)

### Inferences
- Resources for a Jezero base: (a) hydrated minerals such as phyllosilicates, Mg-sulfates, and serpentine, which hold chemically bound water that can be released by heating (an ISRU water source when ice is absent); (b) basaltic and olivine-rich rock for 3D-printing and regolith shielding; (c) sulfates as a possible sulfur source for sulfur concrete; (d) a relatively flat crater floor and delta front for landing and roving. Points (a)–(d) are my synthesis from the abstract plus general ISRU logic, not explicit NASA statements.
- Jezero's lack of near-surface ice at 18°N is a likely weakness compared with mid-latitude ice-rich EZs. The abstract summary did not note water ice. This is my inference.
- Perseverance's MEDA dataset makes Jezero one of the best-characterized sites for weather (temperature, pressure, dust) for future human planning.
