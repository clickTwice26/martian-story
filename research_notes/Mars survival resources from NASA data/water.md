# Water on Mars: where it is, how to get it, and what a crew at Jezero Crater (~18.4°N, 77.5°E) would need

Labels used below: **[MEASURED]** = direct instrument or lab measurement; **[MODEL/ESTIMATE]** = inferred from remote sensing, models or design studies; **[MY CALC]** = my own arithmetic from cited numbers or standard physical constants, not from a source.

---

## 1. Subsurface and near-surface ice: where is it, how deep, how pure?

### Takeaway
Clean, shallow ice is real and confirmed in many places, but only poleward of about 35°N/S. Fresh impact craters expose it at depths under 1 m, eroding scarps show ice sheets more than 100 m thick starting 1–2 m down, and SHARAD radar shows buried glaciers and ice sheets holding thousands of km³. The best mapped "human landing" ice lies at roughly 30–50°N (Arcadia, northern Utopia, Deuteronilus Mensae). Jezero, at 18.4°N, is well equatorward of every confirmed ice exposure.

### Cited Findings
**SWIM (Subsurface Water Ice Mapping) project**
- SWIM is led by the Planetary Science Institute and managed by JPL. It combines data from MRO, Mars Odyssey and Mars Global Surveyor. Its 4th map release (Oct 2023) was the most detailed since the project began in 2017, and the first to use HiRISE plus CTX to trace the ice boundary "as close to the equator as possible." The maps cover the equator to 60°N and show likely ice within the upper 1 m. — [JPL, 2023](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasa-is-locating-ice-on-mars-with-this-new-map/)
- JPL's SWIM project manager says the ideal human landing site is at the southernmost edge of the northern mid-latitude ice region: "If you send humans to Mars, you want to get them as close to the equator as you can." The northern mid-latitudes are also favoured because their thicker atmosphere helps slow landers. — [JPL, 2023](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasa-is-locating-ice-on-mars-with-this-new-map/)
- The amount of ice across the mid-latitudes "isn't uniform; some regions seem to have more than others, and no one really knows why" (N. Putzig, SWIM co-lead). — [JPL, 2023](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasa-is-locating-ice-on-mars-with-this-new-map/)
- **[MODEL/ESTIMATE]** The refined SWIM maps (Morgan et al. 2025, *Planetary Science Journal*) have ~3 km/pixel "ice consistency" (Ci) over depth ranges of 0–1 m, 1–5 m and >5 m. They draw on MONS neutron, TES and MCS thermal, CTX/HiRISE imaging, SHARAD radar and MOLA data. Ci values above the 0.3 threshold extend equatorward to ~30°N and ~20°S. The largest departures from the current ice-stability zone are in Arcadia and northern Utopia Planitia, where Ci >0.5 covers expanses tens of degrees of longitude wide, and in Deuteronilus Mensae. The most equatorward excursions (below 30°) are thought to be out of equilibrium with today's climate. — [Morgan et al. 2025, PSJ](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/ad9b24)
- An earlier SWIM synthesis appeared as Morgan et al. 2021, *Nature Astronomy* 5:230, "Availability of subsurface water-ice resources in the northern mid-latitudes of Mars." — cited in the [Morgan et al. 2025 reference list](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/ad9b24)

**Ice-exposing fresh impact craters (HiRISE)**
- Most ice-exposing craters are ≤10 m across. In 2022 HiRISE imaged a 150 m crater that exposed a large mass of buried ice. — [JPL, 2023](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasa-is-locating-ice-on-mars-with-this-new-map/)
- **[MEASURED]** That 150 m crater near **35°N** is the lowest-latitude impact exposure of subsurface ice on Mars and the largest known ice-exposing crater. The ice is relatively pure but unstable and has nearly disappeared. It may once have been tens of metres thick and extended south of 35°N. It sits near the edge of the shallow ice that exchanges with the atmosphere. The impact (24 Dec 2021) was also recorded by InSight as a magnitude-4 seismic event (S1094b). — [Dundas et al. 2023, GRL, USGS record](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70241592); [USGS "Icy mystery"](https://www.usgs.gov/news/featured-story/icy-mystery)
- **[MEASURED]** Dundas et al. 2021 (JGR Planets) catalogued:
  - **48 ice-exposing impact sites**, all poleward of 35°N/S
  - mean depth to ice of **0.42–0.90 m** across crater clusters, i.e. **<1 m**
  - the lowest-latitude ice exposures at **39.1°N** and **46.2°S** (a probable candidate at 42.6°S)
  - ice with "very low lithic content": as little as ~10 wt% lithics, which is **>95 vol% ice**
  
  The authors describe these sites as reference locations for possible human ISRU. — [Dundas et al. 2021, JGR Planets](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2020JE006617)

**Exposed ice scarps (Dundas et al. 2018, *Science*)**
- **[MEASURED]** Eight eroding scarps in the mid-latitudes expose ice sheets that start just **1–2 m** below the surface and are **>100 m** thick. The scarps are actively retreating as the exposed ice sublimates. The ice probably accumulated as snowfall during past high-obliquity periods. — [Dundas et al. 2018, Science](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aao1619); [USGS summary](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/exposed-subsurface-ice-sheets-martian-mid-latitudes)
- **[MEASURED]** The 2021 follow-up found more scarps:
  - 21 confirmed southern exposures, confined to **50–61°S** and concentrated near 80–115°E
  - more than 70 scarp-like features in Milankovič crater (54.5°N, 213.5°E), with all northern scarps at **53–59°N**
  
  Scarps are "tens of meters high" and plan-view widths are 20–200 m. — [Dundas et al. 2021](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2020JE006617)

**MRO SHARAD radar: buried ice sheets and glaciers**
- **[MODEL/ESTIMATE from radar]** The Utopia Planitia ice deposit (Stuurman et al. 2016, GRL) was mapped from more than 600 SHARAD passes:
  - area larger than New Mexico
  - **80–170 m** thick
  - **50–85% water ice**, the rest dust and rock
  - buried under **~1–10 m** of soil
  - roughly the volume of Lake Superior (~12,000 km³)
  
  This is under 1% of all known Martian water ice, but it more than doubled the known thick buried ice in the northern plains. — [JPL](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/mars-ice-deposit-holds-as-much-water-as-lake-superior/); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/11/161122131031.htm)
- **[MODEL/ESTIMATE from radar]** Lobate debris aprons (LDAs) are mid-latitude glaciers. Holt et al. 2008 (*Science* 322:1235, "Radar sounding evidence for buried glaciers in the southern mid-latitudes of Mars") interpreted SHARAD reflections from the top and base of the Hellas-region aprons as mostly near-pure ice under a thin rock-debris cover. All LDAs examined in Deuteronilus Mensae contain ice. — [Planetary Society summary](https://www.planetary.org/articles/1766); [UT Jackson School](https://www.jsg.utexas.edu/news/2008/11/imagery-video-for-mars-glaciers-science-paper-by-holt-et-al-nov-21-2008/)
- Holt (press statement): these glaciers "almost certainly represent the largest reservoir of water ice on Mars that is not in the polar caps." — [UT Jackson School](https://www.jsg.utexas.edu/news/2008/11/imagery-video-for-mars-glaciers-science-paper-by-holt-et-al-nov-21-2008/)
- LDAs are reported as ">80% pure ice". This number is only from Wikipedia's summary, and I did not verify it in a primary source. — [Wikipedia: Lobate debris apron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lobate_debris_apron)
- A regional survey (Petersen, Holt & Levy 2018, GRL 45:11,595) is titled "High Ice Purity of Martian Lobate Debris Aprons at the Regional Scale" (Deuteronilus/Protonilus Mensae). — cited in the [Morgan et al. 2025 reference list](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/ad9b24)

**Phoenix lander (68°N)**
- **[MEASURED]** The ice table at the Phoenix site was ~**5 cm** deep, and soil chemistry at the surface and at the ice boundary did not differ significantly. — [Hecht et al. 2009, Science (abstract)](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/detection-of-perchlorate-and-the-soluble-chemistry-of-martian-soi/)
- Trenching found ice only a couple of inches down under polygon centres. In the troughs between polygons the team dug ~8 inches (~20 cm) without reaching ice. These figures come from press interviews, not the paper. — [Spaceflight Now, 2009](https://spaceflightnow.com/news/n0907/02phoenix/)

**Polar caps and layered deposits**
- **[MODEL/ESTIMATE from radar]** The south polar layered deposits (MARSIS, Plaut et al. 2007) hold ~**1.6 × 10⁶ km³**, mostly water ice. That equals a global water layer ~**11 m** deep. — [Plaut et al. 2007, Science (PDF)](https://space.physics.uiowa.edu/~dag/publications/2007_SubsurfaceRadarSoundingOfTheSouthPolarLayeredDepositsOfMars_science.pdf)
- **[MODEL/ESTIMATE]** Buried ice beneath the north polar cap (SHARAD, 2019) would form a global layer at least **1.5 m** deep. The study calls it likely the third-largest water reservoir on Mars after the polar caps. — [ScienceDaily / UT Austin, 2019](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2019/05/190522141747.htm)
- JPL notes the poles have plenty of ice but are "too cold for astronauts (or robots) to survive for long." — [JPL, 2023](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasa-is-locating-ice-on-mars-with-this-new-map/)

**New (2026) work**
- A Feb 2026 report (Astrobiology.com, summarising a new paper) says recent observations show the near-surface ice layer reaches **35°** latitude and interprets mid-latitude ice as the remnant of a past ice sheet. I saw only the search snippet, not the full text. — [Astrobiology.com, 25 Feb 2026](https://astrobiology.com/2026/02/25/the-martian-mid-latitude-subsurface-ice-is-the-remnant-of-a-past-ice-sheet/)

### Inferences
- **[MY CALC]** On Mars one degree of latitude is ≈59 km (radius ≈3,390 km). From Jezero (18.4°N), reaching the ~30°N SWIM Ci>0.3 boundary means travelling at least ~11.6° ≈ **~690 km**. Reaching the lowest-latitude confirmed ice (35°N) means at least ~16.6° ≈ **~980 km**. These are minimum due-north distances; the confirmed 35°N crater is at a very different longitude, so the true distance to *confirmed* ice is larger.
- For a survival story: shallow ice is a "dig 0.5–1 m with a shovel" resource at 39–60°N. At Jezero it is not available locally, and any ice there would have to be inferred, not mapped.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve a SWIM Ci value or map tile for the Isidis/Jezero region. The PSJ abstract only gives the ~30°N equatorward limit.
- I did not find a primary-source ice percentage for LDAs. The ">80%" figure is unverified, and the Petersen et al. 2018 numbers were not retrieved.
- Phoenix's 68°N latitude is the commonly cited value but was not re-verified in this session's sources.

## 2. Water bound in regolith and minerals (soil, sulfates, clays, perchlorates)

### Takeaway
Even "dry" Martian dirt holds a few percent water. Curiosity measured ~1.5–3 wt% in Gale soil (released by heating), and Odyssey neutron data imply ~2–10% water-equivalent hydrogen (WEH) in equatorial regolith. Sulfate deposits are the rich ore: gypsum is ~20 wt% water and releases it at ~150°C.

### Cited Findings
- **[MEASURED] Leshin et al. 2013 (Science), verified.** Curiosity SAM heated Rocknest aeolian fines to ~835°C. H₂O, SO₂, CO₂ and O₂ were the major gases released. Water abundance was **1.5–3 weight percent**, and the release temperatures suggest the water is bound in an amorphous component of the sample. — [Caltech record of Leshin et al. 2013](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/s584q-ekz80); [NASA-hosted PDF](https://mars.nasa.gov/files/msl/Science-2013-Leshin-.pdf)
- **[MEASURED]** A companion JGR analysis (Archer et al. 2014) found water released from Rocknest averaged **2.0 ± 1.3%** across four SAM runs. — [JGR Planets 10.1002/2013JE004493](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/2013je004493)
- **[MODEL/ESTIMATE] Mars Odyssey Neutron Spectrometer (Feldman et al. 2004).** Equatorial deposits between ±45° latitude range **2–10% WEH by mass**, peaking in two regions that straddle the 0 km elevation contour. Up to **~11%** is needed in some equatorial subsurface regolith if the top 10 g/cm² is desiccated. — [Feldman et al. 2004, JGR (abstract)](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/global-distribution-of-near-surface-hydrogen-on-mars/)
- Reprocessed Odyssey data (Wilson et al., 2017–18) show more equatorial WEH than the raw data. Water ice is not thought to be thermodynamically stable at any depth at low latitudes, so excess equatorial hydrogen was first attributed to hydrated minerals. The neutron spectrometer measures hydrogen, not water directly. — [JHU APL press release, 2017](https://www.jhuapl.edu/news/news-releases/170928-fresh-look-older-mars-data-yields-equatorial-surprise)
- A NASA Glenn regolith-extraction paper summarises Mars near-surface regolith as containing "anywhere from 2–10 percent water by mass" (citing Abbud-Madrid 2016 and others), and 3–13% H₂O/OH⁻ in the top ~100 μm (Audouard 2014). — [Trunek et al., NTRS 20180002377](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20180002377/downloads/20180002377.pdf)
- **[MODEL/ESTIMATE] Feedstock grades and release temperatures** from the NASA/MEPAG M-WIP study (Abbud-Madrid et al. 2016):

  | Feedstock | Water content | Release temperature |
  |---|---|---|
  | Typical regolith (Case D, modelled on Rocknest and conservative DAN results) | **1.5 wt%** | — |
  | Smectite clay | **~4 wt%** | 300°C (573 K) |
  | Poly-hydrated sulfate / gypsum | **~20 wt%** | 100–150°C (423 K) |
  | Glacial ice deposit (reference) | **90% ice** | — |
  | Allophane | — | 363 K |
  | Bassanite | — | 423 K |
  | Akaganeite | — | 523 K |
  | Basaltic glass | — | >750 K |

  — [M-WIP report (PDF)](https://sciences.ucf.edu/class/wp-content/uploads/sites/23/2020/04/Mars_Water_ISRU_Stud1.pdf)
- **Perchlorate (Phoenix) [MEASURED].** Wet Chemistry Lab leachates held ~10 mM dissolved salts with **0.4–0.6% perchlorate (ClO₄⁻) by mass**, at pH 7.7 ± 0.5 (carbonate-buffered). — [Hecht et al. 2009 (abstract)](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/detection-of-perchlorate-and-the-soluble-chemistry-of-martian-soi/)
- **Perchlorate brines.** Calcium perchlorate has the lowest eutectic of the deliquescent salts (**198 K**), which sets the lowest temperature for liquid brine on present-day Mars. Modelling suggests present Jezero conditions allow hydration of sulfates, chlorides and perchlorates. — [Sci. Reports 2024, s41598-024-57458-4](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41598-024-57458-4)

### Inferences
- **[MY CALC]** At 1.5 wt%, one kg of water needs ~67 kg of regolith processed. At 20 wt% gypsum it needs only ~5 kg. This 13× difference in mass handled is why M-WIP favours sulfate deposits.
- Gale "1.5–3 wt%" is a measured, local value. Odyssey "2–10% WEH" is a model-based, ~hundreds-of-km footprint value that includes hydrated minerals and adsorbed water.

### Gaps
- I did not find a published Odyssey WEH value specifically for the Jezero/Isidis pixel.

## 3. Jezero specifically: hydrated minerals and distance to ice

### Takeaway
Jezero is rich in water-*altered* rock: carbonates, Fe/Mg clays, hydrated Mg/Fe sulfates (up to ~20 wt% SO₃ in fan-front mudstone), hydrated silica/opal, and Cl-rich regolith (up to 2 wt% Cl, possibly perchlorate). It has **no known ice**. Perseverance's ground-penetrating radar sees solid mafic rock. Water at Jezero would have to come from cooking hydrated minerals or regolith, or be brought in.

### Cited Findings
- **[MEASURED] Western fan samples.**
  - All sandstones are **hydrated** and contain **Fe/Mg carbonates** and aqueously altered olivine and pyroxene.
  - Fan-front mudstone and sandstone contain **clay minerals** and **hydrated Mg/Fe sulfates (up to 20 wt% SO₃)**.
  - The sulfate-bearing mudstone is the best biosignature candidate.
  
  — [Bosak et al., AbSciCon 2024 / AGU Advances (NTRS)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240000548/downloads/Bosak%20AbSciCon%202024.pdf)
- **[MEASURED] SuperCam (Clavé et al.).** Carbonates and olivine-bearing rocks occur throughout Jezero and are generally inversely correlated. Carbonates correlate positively with hydrated silica. — [Eos research spotlight](https://eos.org/research-spotlights/carbon-rich-rocks-may-have-cooled-the-ancient-martian-atmosphere)
- **[MEASURED] SuperCam (Beck et al. 2025, EPSL).** Hydrated silica (opal/chalcedony) cobbles and the first quartz-dominated stones seen on the Martian surface, with a possible hydrothermal origin. They were found as float, not in place. — [USGS record](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70263947)
- **[MEASURED] Mostly unhydrated sulfate in places.** SuperCam Raman found sulfate at the Wildcat Ridge and Hidden Harbor outcrops without hydration bands (3200–3700 cm⁻¹), indicating **anhydrite** there. Sulfate hydration states on Mars remain poorly constrained. — [arXiv 2410.05615](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.05615)
- **[MEASURED] Regolith (PIXL).** Western fan-front regolith has fewer sulfates but is enriched in **Cl, up to 2.0 ± 0.5 wt%** (chlorides, chlorates and/or perchlorates). PIXL also sees hydration carried by salts, interstitial water and/or adsorbed phases. — [Astrobiology.com summary of JGR paper, Oct 2025](https://astrobiology.com/2025/10/11/mineralogically-diverse-and-salt-rich-regolith-in-jezero-crater-characterized-using-x-ray-spectroscopy/); [Stony Brook record](https://researchconnect.stonybrook.edu/en/publications/mineralogically-diverse-and-salt-rich-regolith-in-jezero-crater-c/)
- **[MEASURED] RIMFAX radar (Hamran et al. 2022, Science Advances).** Strong reflectors in the Séitah formation have permittivity consistent with low-porosity mafic rock, in tilted layers. A 2023 analysis (Casademont et al., EGU) supports a solid-rock, mafic interpretation of the shallow subsurface. — [UCLA, 2022](https://www.college.ucla.edu/2022/08/25/mars-underground-images-from-perseverance-rover); [EGU23-15500](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU23/EGU23-15500.html)
- Before landing, RIMFAX deputy PI David Paige said he did not expect the ground under the rover to be wet, since any shallow water at Jezero would be frozen. — [UCLA Q&A, 2021](https://www.college.ucla.edu/2021/03/12/qa-david-paige-on-the-mars-perseverance-landing)
- Ice is not thermodynamically stable at any depth at low latitudes. — [JHU APL, 2017](https://www.jhuapl.edu/news/news-releases/170928-fresh-look-older-mars-data-yields-equatorial-surprise)
- SWIM Ci >0.3 extends only to ~30°N, and the lowest confirmed ice exposures are at 35°N (crater) and 39.1°N (2021 catalogue). — [Morgan et al. 2025](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/ad9b24); [Dundas et al. 2023](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70241592); [Dundas et al. 2021](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2020JE006617)
- Modelling suggests a **3-billion-year-old** polythermal ice sheet once covered Isidis Planitia, the basin next to Jezero (Souček et al. 2015, EPSL 426:176). This is ancient and modelled, not evidence of present-day ice. — cited in the [Morgan et al. 2025 reference list](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/ad9b24)
- Shean 2010 (GRL) proposed "candidate ice-rich material within equatorial craters." I have only the title, not the locations or confirmation. — cited in the [Morgan et al. 2025 reference list](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/ad9b24)

### Inferences
- **Nearest expected usable ground ice:** at least ~700 km (to the ~30°N SWIM edge) and more realistically ≥1,000 km (to 35–40°N confirmed exposures). Depth there would be <1 m in crater exposures, or 1–10 m over thick ice sheets. **[MY CALC from cited latitudes]**
- At Jezero, the best local water ores are:
  1. **hydrated Mg/Fe sulfate mudstones** of the western fan front (highest-grade mineral feedstock)
  2. **clays** in the fan and floor
  3. ubiquitous regolith at ~1.5–3 wt% (Gale analogue)
  
  Some sulfate is anhydrite (no water), so ore would need to be prospected.
- Cl-rich Jezero regolith (up to 2 wt% Cl) means any water baked out of local soil should be presumed perchlorate- and chlorate-contaminated.

### Gaps
- I found no published wt% H₂O for Jezero crater-floor or fan sulfates or clays (hydration states poorly constrained).
- I found no publication giving a "nearest ice to Jezero" distance or a SWIM pixel value there. The distances above are my estimates.

## 4. Extraction methods and energy cost

### Takeaway
NASA's M-WIP study sized a plant to make **16 t of water in 480 sols** (mostly for ascent-vehicle propellant). Processing power ranged from **~1.6 kW** (gypsum at 150°C) to **~7.4–7.6 kW** (typical 1.5 wt% regolith), which works out to roughly **1–6 kWh per kg of water**. Ice cases were judged lowest in mass and energy if access problems are solved. Pulling water from the atmosphere (210 ppm) would need **~65 MW** for that demand.

### Cited Findings
**NASA/MEPAG Mars Water ISRU Planning (M-WIP) study, Abbud-Madrid et al. 2016** — [M-WIP report (PDF)](https://sciences.ucf.edu/class/wp-content/uploads/sites/23/2020/04/Mars_Water_ISRU_Stud1.pdf); [ResearchGate entry](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/301614744_Mars_Water_In-Situ_Resource_Utilization_ISRU_Planning_M-WIP_Study)
- **[MODEL/ESTIMATE] Demand and schedule.**
  - Reference demand: **16 metric tons of water** per crew mission, for a fully fuelled Mars Ascent Vehicle (MAV) plus crew-life-support oxygen.
  - Production window: **480 days**. The ISRU plant lands one opportunity ahead of the crew and makes all propellant before crew launch.
  - It also cites a separate requirement of **5 t of water per year** (from the HLS2 workshop).
- **[MODEL/ESTIMATE] Processing power for granular feedstocks** (continuous soil reactor, MAV propellant demand):

  | Case | Feedstock | Power | Mass processed in 480 sols |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | D1 | Typical regolith @150°C | **7.4 kW** | **~2,020 t** |
  | D2 | Typical regolith @300°C | **7.6 kW** | **~1,250 t** |
  | B | Gypsum @150°C | **1.6 kW** | **~180 t** |
  | C | Smectite @300°C | **4.8 kW** | **~580 t** |

- **[MODEL/ESTIMATE] Whole-plant power** (water plus electrolysis, Sabatier and liquefaction):
  - **~22 kW total** with sulfate feedstock: regolith thermal 3.5 kW, electrolysis 23.5 kW line item, CH₄ liquefaction 3 kW, O₂ liquefaction 3.5 kW, atmosphere processing 3 kW
  - **~28 kW total** with typical regolith: regolith thermal 17.5 kW
  
  Line items as extracted do not sum exactly to the stated totals; check the original table before quoting.
- **[MODEL/ESTIMATE] Atmospheric water extraction** as an alternative for this demand: **~65 megawatts**.
- **Ice cases.**
  - Reference deposit: 90% ice under 1–10 m of overburden (the range limited by SHARAD resolution).
  - **Case A1** is an open pit. Overburden and ramp mass to move rises geometrically with depth to ice, per 480 sols: 40 t (1 m), 160 t (2 m), 350 t (3 m), 680 t (4 m), 1,150 t (5 m), 1,750 t (6 m), 2,250 t (7 m).
  - **Case A2** is "down-hole" in-situ recovery (drill/kerf, cryobot heat probe, subsurface heating, gas-phase recovery with a cold trap).
  - Power for the ice cases was not established (TBD).
- **Conclusions.**
  - Excluding the down-hole case, a **poly-hydrated sulfate deposit (Case B) is the most advantageous** reference case.
  - If challenges are resolved, the subsurface ice cases (A1, A2) would need less mass and energy than any mineral case.
  - The human landing-site constraint used was **equatorward of 50°** latitude.

**Rodwell ("Rodriguez well") / RedWater**
- Honeybee Robotics' **RedWater** combines coiled-tubing drilling with a **Rodriguez well (RodWell)**: drill into ice, melt a subsurface water pool, pump the water up. It reached **TRL 5** in end-to-end tests in −60°C ice at Mars ambient pressure (Mellerowicz et al., *New Space*, 2022). — [Michigan Tech Digital Commons: RedWater](https://digitalcommons.mtu.edu/michigantech-p/16804); [RedWater: A Rodwell system](https://digitalcommons.mtu.edu/michigantech-p/15790)
- No kWh/kg figure for RedWater was found in the abstracts.

**Regolith baking experiments (NASA Glenn)**
- **[MEASURED, lab]** Trunek et al. ran an open "tilt-tray" reactor with GRC-3 simulant spiked to **4.2 wt%** water using borax decahydrate. The tray was at ≥150°C with a soil target of 100°C. Water collection efficiency was **~50% (45–58%)**, and the condenser alone was 85.3% efficient with liquid water. Energy per kg was not reported. — [NTRS 20180002377](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20180002377/downloads/20180002377.pdf)

**Atmospheric water (WAVAR and others)**
- **[MEASURED]** The Mars atmosphere contains **210 ppm H₂O**. Mean surface pressure is 6.36 mb (range 4.0–8.7 mb). Mean temperature is ~214 K, and the diurnal range at Viking 1 was 184–242 K. — [NASA NSSDC Mars Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html)
- WAVAR (water-vapour adsorption reactor) blows air over a zeolite bed and then heats the bed to release the water. It is mechanically simple and works anywhere, but its output is too low for propellant, so it is seen at most as a life-support supplement. — [Wikipedia: WAVAR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WAVAR); [Strathclyde review](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/94675/)
- An unverified forum figure (not a primary source) cites a WAVAR design making 45 kg/sol on 15 kW with 885 kg of hardware, operating only in summer. — [New Mars Forums](https://newmars.com/forums/viewtopic.php?id=7680)

### Inferences
- **[MY CALC] Energy per kg from M-WIP.** Assume the listed power runs continuously for 480 sols (1 sol ≈ 24.66 h, so ~11,840 h) to make 16,000 kg:

  | Feedstock | Total energy | Per kg of water |
  |---|---|---|
  | Typical regolith @150°C (7.4 kW) | ≈ 87,600 kWh | **≈ 5.5 kWh/kg** |
  | Typical regolith @300°C (7.6 kW) | — | **≈ 5.6 kWh/kg** |
  | Smectite (4.8 kW) | — | **≈ 3.6 kWh/kg** |
  | Gypsum (1.6 kW) | — | **≈ 1.2 kWh/kg** |

  Production rate is ≈ **33 kg water/sol**.
- **[MY CALC, standard physical constants, not sourced]** Thermodynamic floor for ice:
  - Sublimation (latent heat ~2.83 MJ/kg) costs ≈ **0.8 kWh/kg**.
  - Melting from ~200 K (warm ice ~73 K at ~2 kJ/kg·K, plus 334 kJ/kg fusion, plus warming to ~20°C) costs ≈ **0.15 kWh/kg** before losses.
  
  So mined ice beats regolith by roughly 10–40× in energy.
- **[MY CALC, standard constants]** Regolith check: heating 67 kg of 1.5 wt% soil from ~210 K to 423 K (cp ~0.8 kJ/kg·K) is ≈ 11 MJ ≈ **3 kWh** per kg of water before dehydration enthalpy or heat recovery. That is consistent with the M-WIP-derived ~5.5 kWh/kg.
- **[MY CALC]** Atmosphere: 210 ppm by volume × (18/44 molar mass ratio) ≈ 86 ppm by mass. Getting 1 kg of water means processing ≈ **11–12 t of Martian air**, even at 100% capture. This is why NASA's estimate is in the megawatts for propellant-scale demand.
- For a stranded-survivor story at Jezero: baking hydrated sulfate mudstone at ~150°C is the most energy-efficient local option (~1 kWh/kg). Plain soil costs ~5 kWh/kg. A crew's 2.5 L/day minimum would need ~3–14 kWh/day per person depending on feedstock (my estimate).

### Gaps
- I found no measured kWh/kg for RodWell/RedWater or for a primary-source WAVAR design (Grover & Bruckner 1998; Coons et al. 1997 AIAA not retrieved).
- M-WIP ice-case power was TBD in the study itself.

## 5. Water purification: perchlorates and toxicity

### Takeaway
Martian soil water will carry perchlorate at percent levels: 0.4–0.6% in Phoenix soil, and Jezero regolith holds up to 2 wt% Cl as chloride, chlorate or perchlorate. Perchlorate blocks thyroid iodide uptake, so water must be cleaned by roughly 10⁵× before drinking. Candidate methods are distillation or condensation (which leaves salts behind), ion exchange, microbial reduction and catalytic reduction.

### Cited Findings
- **[MEASURED]** Phoenix soil: 0.4–0.6 wt% perchlorate; ~10 mM soluble salts; pH 7.7. — [Hecht et al. 2009](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/detection-of-perchlorate-and-the-soluble-chemistry-of-martian-soi/)
- "Perchlorate was not predicted at this landing site, and nobody had it on their list of likely chemicals" (Phoenix PI Peter Smith). — [Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/news/n0907/02phoenix/)
- **[MEASURED]** Jezero fan-front regolith: Cl up to 2.0 ± 0.5 wt%, likely including perchlorates and chlorates. — [Astrobiology.com summary](https://astrobiology.com/2025/10/11/mineralogically-diverse-and-salt-rich-regolith-in-jezero-crater-characterized-using-x-ray-spectroscopy/)
- Perchlorate competes with iodide uptake in the thyroid and lowers thyroid function. Chronic low-level exposure can cause thyroid inhibition and lung toxicity. — [NASA NTRS 20190028297](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20190028297/downloads/20190028297.pdf); [Space.com](https://space.com/21554-mars-toxic-perchlorate-chemicals.html)
- **[MODEL/ESTIMATE]** Rothschild et al. (LPSC 56, 2025, "Detoxifying Mars") estimate raw Martian water will contain **~10,000,000 ppb** each of perchlorate and chlorate, requiring reduction by **~100,000×**. A NASA Ames project is engineering yeast to reduce perchlorate to chloride plus oxygen. The link is a third-party mirror of the LPSC abstract. — [Rothschild et al. 2025 (mirror)](https://epizodyspace.ru/bibl/inostr-yazyki/Lunar_and_Planetary_Science/2025/Rothschild_et_al_Detoxifying_Mars_56th_LPSC_no_2713_(2025).pdf)
- **[MEASURED, lab]** A UC Riverside molybdenum catalyst reduces **>99.99%** of perchlorate to chloride regardless of starting concentration. This is a lab result, not flight hardware. — [NSF](https://www.nsf.gov/news/new-water-treatment-earth-could-also-help-mars)
- Perchlorate in dust is low enough that filtration mitigates the inhalation risk. Water is a bigger problem. Perchlorate is also a potential oxygen source. — [NASA NTRS 20150023545](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150023545/downloads/20150023545.pdf); [Astronomy.com](https://www.astronomy.com/space-exploration/salts-on-mars-could-pose-unseen-hazards-to-explorers/)

### Inferences
- Thermal extraction that evaporates water and condenses it elsewhere (M-WIP style cold traps, Rodwell vapour recovery) should leave non-volatile perchlorate salts behind in the hot residue. Any liquid leaching of soil, or melting of salty ice, would carry dissolved perchlorate into the water. Clean mid-latitude ice (>95 vol% ice) is likely far lower in perchlorate than soil, but I found no measurement.
- Some HCl and SO₂ co-evolve when soil is heated (SAM saw SO₂ as a major gas), so condensate from regolith heating may be acidic and need polishing. This is an inference from Leshin 2013's evolved-gas list.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve a NASA spacecraft water-quality limit (SWEG) or EPA reference dose for perchlorate.
- I found no measured perchlorate content of mid-latitude ice.

## 6. How much water does a crew need?

### Takeaway
NASA's standard is a minimum of **2.5 L of potable water per crewmember per day** for drinking and food, plus **240 mL per EVA hour**. Hygiene is mission-dependent. ISS crews use about a gallon (~3.8 L) per person per day in total, and the station recovers ~**98%** of it. With good recycling, a crew's make-up need is small; the big ISRU driver is propellant (16 t per mission).

### Cited Findings
- **[REQUIREMENT]** NASA OCHMO water technical brief (NASA-STD-3001):
  - potable water for hydration: **minimum 2.5 L (84.5 fl oz) per crewmember per day**, including 600 mL per meal available as hot water
  - EVA: **minimum 240 mL (8.1 fl oz) per EVA hour**
  - hygiene water: mission dependent
  
  — [NASA OCHMO Water Technical Brief (2023)](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/water-technical-brief-ochmo.pdf)
- **[REQUIREMENT, older]** A 2018 NASA TFAWS presentation, citing NASA-STD-3001, lists drinking at 2 kg, food hydration at 0.5 kg and personal hygiene at 0.4 kg per crewmember-day. — [NTRS 20180002051](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20180002051/downloads/20180002051.pdf)
- **[MODEL/ESTIMATE]** Life Support BVAD (NASA/TP-2015-218570) values for a mature planetary base, as reproduced in a NASA presentation: hand washing ~4.08 kg, showers ~2.72 kg, oral hygiene ~0.37 kg, for a hygiene subtotal of ~7.17 kg per crewmember-day. — [NTRS 20170001718](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170001718/downloads/20170001718.pdf)
- **[MEASURED]** ISS experience: each astronaut needs ~1 gallon per day for drinking, food prep and hygiene. The Water Recovery System recovers and recycles ~**98%** of station water. — [NASA ECLSS fact sheet](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/g-657270-59-hp-environmental-control-and-life-support-system-eclss.pdf)
- Before the Brine Processor Assembly was added, recovery was ~93–94%. The BPA reclaims residual water from urine-distillation brine. — [ABC7, 2023](https://abc7.com/13433546/)
- Measured ISS intake (drinking plus food rehydration) runs ~2–2.3 L per person per day across expeditions. This is a search-summary figure from an SAE ISS water-usage analysis that I did not open. — [SAE 2008-01-2009](https://saemobilus.sae.org/papers/international-space-station-water-usage-analysis-2008-01-2009)
- **[MODEL/ESTIMATE]** M-WIP: 16 t of water per mission, mostly for MAV propellant (O₂/CH₄), plus crew O₂; another requirement cited is 5 t/year. — [M-WIP](https://sciences.ucf.edu/class/wp-content/uploads/sites/23/2020/04/Mars_Water_ISRU_Stud1.pdf)

### Inferences
- **[MY CALC]** Four crew using ~3.8 kg/person/day (ISS) is ~15 kg/day in circulation. At 98% recovery the loss is ~0.3 kg/day, or ~150 kg over a 500-sol surface stay. With no recycling, the same crew would need ~7.6 t over 500 sols. Using the BVAD planetary-base hygiene allowance (~7 kg) plus 2.5 kg potable gives ~9.7 kg/person/day, or ~19 t open-loop for 4 crew over 500 sols.
- **[MY CALC]** Survival minimum: 2.5 L/person/day × 4 crew = 10 kg/day. Baked from typical Jezero-like regolith (1.5 wt%, ~50% collection efficiency per the NASA Glenn tests), that is ~1.3 t of soil per day. From 20 wt% sulfate it is ~0.1 t per day.

### Gaps
- I did not open the full current BVAD (2015 TP or 2022 update) to confirm per-day totals. The figures above are reproductions in NASA presentations.
