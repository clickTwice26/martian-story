## WHO · 0:00–0:45

Imagine waking up alone on Mars, after a crash, with less than two hours of oxygen in your suit. Where would you find air, water and shelter?

We are Team Butterfly, from Cumilla, Bangladesh: Shagato Chowdhury, Tamima Rahman Choity, Moitry Bhowmik Puja, Md. Raihanul Hasan, Saifullah Ibne Reza, and Umma Iman Monea.

Six people, one goal: to make the real Mars something anyone can explore, not just read about.

For the NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026, we built Martian Story: a walkable, survivable map of Jezero Crater, the place where NASA's Perseverance rover landed, made entirely from NASA's open data.

## WHY · 0:45–1:45

Our challenge is the Interplanetary Survival Guide: Martian Map. Future crews will need to know a place on Mars before they land: where the ground is safe to walk, what the rocks are made of, how cold the nights get, and what it takes to make air and water.

That knowledge already exists. NASA has mapped Jezero in astonishing detail, and Perseverance has driven more than forty-five kilometres across it. But it is scattered across scientific archives, in formats only specialists can open.

Here is how precise it is. At the Perseverance landing site, our terrain reads minus two thousand five hundred and sixty-nine point nine two metres. NASA's recorded value is point nine one. One centimetre apart.

We wanted anyone, a student, a teacher, a future astronaut, to stand on that ground and understand it.

## WHAT · 1:45–2:45

Martian Story puts that place in a web browser. The terrain is the USGS Mars 2020 HiRISE digital terrain model, one metre per pixel, shaded by the HiRISE orthomosaic. The crater rim comes from the CTX elevation model and the MOLA laser altimeter.

Press K, and minerals measured by the MRO CRISM spectrometer light up the ground: olivine, carbonate, and hydrated minerals that could give up water.

The blue line is Perseverance's real traverse, from NASA's MMGIS maps. Press P, and you see the Mastcam-Z and Navcam photos the rover took right here, its RIMFAX radar showing the layers underground, and the weather from MEDA, its own weather station: right now, minus sixty degrees, at seven hundred pascals.

And there is Perseverance itself, NASA's own 3D model, parked on its real waypoint. Eleven NASA datasets, working together, in one place.

## IMPACT · 2:45–3:45

Every survival rule follows the science. Oxygen comes from a MOXIE-style electrolyser, water is baked out of sulfate minerals, power follows the real Martian sun from NASA's Mars24 clock, and radiation is counted against Curiosity's dose measurements. Fourteen missions take you from your first breath to a launch home.

Students learn Mars by walking on it. Teachers get a free classroom tool that needs no install. And anyone can see NASA's data, together, in one place.

Next, we want more landing sites, a guided classroom mode, and multiplayer crews who plan a base together.
